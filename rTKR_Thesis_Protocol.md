## 2. INTRODUCTION

Total knee replacement (TKR) is one of the most successful and widely performed orthopaedic procedures for end-stage degenerative and inflammatory arthritis of the knee joint. The procedure provides predictable pain relief, functional improvement, and enhanced quality of life for the majority of patients. Over 1.5 million primary TKR procedures are performed annually worldwide, and this number is projected to increase substantially over the coming decades.

With the exponential rise in primary TKR procedures, the burden of revision total knee replacement (rTKR) has also increased significantly. Kurtz et al. (2007) projected that revision TKR procedures in the United States will increase by 601% by the year 2030, from approximately 38,300 procedures in 2005 to an estimated 268,200 procedures annually. This rising revision burden is attributed to the increasing number of primary TKRs performed in younger and more active patients, longer life expectancy, higher functional demands, and improved surveillance detecting early failures.

Revision TKR is indicated in cases of aseptic loosening, periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), instability, polyethylene wear, periprosthetic fracture, malalignment, arthrofibrosis, and extensor mechanism failure. Among these, aseptic loosening and infection remain the most common indications, together accounting for nearly 50–60% of all revision procedures worldwide.

Revision knee surgery is technically more demanding than primary TKR due to several challenges including bone loss, soft tissue compromise, ligamentous insufficiency, loss of anatomical landmarks, and the need for higher-constraint implants. Management of bone defects requires meticulous preoperative planning and intraoperative decision-making, and may necessitate the use of metal augments, metaphyseal sleeves, tantalum cones, structural allografts, or impaction bone grafting. The choice of implant constraint—ranging from posterior-stabilized to constrained condylar to rotating hinge—depends on the degree of ligamentous insufficiency, bone loss, and soft tissue status.

Despite significant advances in implant design, surgical techniques, and perioperative protocols, the outcomes of revision TKR remain inferior to primary TKR with respect to pain relief, functional recovery, and patient satisfaction. Published literature consistently reports lower functional scores, higher complication rates, and lower patient satisfaction rates following revision TKR compared to primary procedures. Patient satisfaction rates of 70–85% have been reported following revision TKR compared to approximately 90% following primary TKR.

Comprehensive evaluation of outcomes following revision TKR requires a multidimensional approach encompassing clinical assessment (range of motion, stability, pain, function), radiological parameters (component alignment, radiolucent lines, signs of loosening, restoration of joint line), and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) including satisfaction scores. Such comprehensive evaluation helps identify factors associated with good and poor outcomes and guides future clinical decision-making.

There is a significant paucity of Indian data from high-volume tertiary care centres regarding comprehensive outcomes following revision TKR. Most published literature originates from Western populations with potentially different demographics, disease patterns, implant usage, and patient expectations compared to the Indian population. Notably, data from emerging economies suggests that infection may be a substantially more dominant cause of revision than in Western registries, highlighting the need for region-specific evidence. No published data exists from KEM Hospital, one of the highest-volume arthroplasty centres in Maharashtra.

This study aims to evaluate the clinico-radiological outcomes and patient satisfaction following revision total knee replacement performed at the Department of Orthopaedics, Seth G.S. Medical College and KEM Hospital, Mumbai.

## 3. AIM & OBJECTIVES

**AIM:** To evaluate the clinico-radiological outcomes and patient satisfaction following revision total knee replacement.

**OBJECTIVES:**

1. To study the demographic profile and indications for revision total knee replacement in our patient cohort.
2. To assess the clinical outcomes of revision TKR using validated scoring systems—the Knee Society Score (KSS 2011) and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC).
3. To evaluate the radiological outcomes including component alignment, radiolucent lines, and signs of loosening using the Knee Society Roentgenographic Evaluation and Scoring System.
4. To assess patient satisfaction following revision TKR using a validated patient satisfaction questionnaire.
5. To identify factors influencing clinical and radiological outcomes including age, sex, BMI, indication for revision, degree of bone loss (AORI classification), type of implant constraint, use of stems and augments, and single-stage versus two-stage revision.
6. To document the complications and re-revision rates in the study cohort and to perform implant survivorship analysis.

## 4. RESEARCH QUESTION & HYPOTHESIS

**RESEARCH QUESTION:** What are the clinical, radiological, and patient satisfaction outcomes following revision total knee replacement at a high-volume tertiary care centre in India, and which patient-, surgery-, and implant-related factors predict poorer outcomes?

**HYPOTHESIS:**

- **Null Hypothesis (H0):** Revision total knee replacement does not result in a statistically significant improvement in clinical and functional outcome scores from the preoperative state.
- **Alternative Hypothesis (H1):** Revision total knee replacement results in a statistically significant improvement in clinical and functional outcome scores, with patient satisfaction comparable to published international data, and specific identifiable factors influence the outcomes.

### 5.5 SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATION

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome—the change in the Knee Society Score (KSS) from the preoperative state to final follow-up. Because this is a **paired** (pre- versus post-operative) comparison within the same cohort, the sample size was estimated using the formula for a paired difference of means:

```
n = [(Z₁₋α/₂ + Z₁₋β)² × σ_d²] / d²
```

Where:

- **Z₁₋α/₂ = 1.96** (two-sided 95% confidence level, α = 0.05)
- **Z₁₋β = 0.84** (80% power)
- **d** = minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in KSS to be detected
- **σ_d** = standard deviation of the *change* in KSS

This expression can be written equivalently in terms of the standardized effect size (Cohen's *d*), where **ES = d / σ_d**:

```
n = (Z₁₋α/₂ + Z₁₋β)² / ES²
```

**Choice of effect size.** Baier et al. (2013), in a patient-oriented evaluation of revision TKR, reported a mean improvement in KSS of approximately 34 points. Taking a clinically meaningful difference (MCID) of 10 KSS points against a standard deviation of change of approximately 19.6 points yields a **conservative standardized effect size of 0.51**:

```
ES = d / σ_d = 10 / 19.6 ≈ 0.51
```

**Calculation:**

```
n = (1.96 + 0.84)² / (0.51)²
  = (2.80)² / 0.260
  = 7.84 / 0.260
  ≈ 30.1
  ≈ 30 evaluable patients
```

Therefore, a minimum of **30 evaluable patients** (i.e., patients with complete primary-outcome follow-up) is required to detect a clinically meaningful improvement in KSS with **80% power** at a **5% two-sided significance level**.

> **Sensitivity note.** At the slightly more conservative effect size of 0.50, 30 evaluable patients still provide approximately **78% power**, and 32 evaluable patients provide 80% power. Given that the *observed* mean improvement reported by Baier et al. (~34 points) corresponds to a far larger true effect size, the study is comfortably powered for the anticipated improvement; the figure above represents a deliberately conservative lower bound based on the MCID rather than the expected effect.

**Adjustment for loss to follow-up.** Anticipating approximately 20% attrition (loss to follow-up, withdrawal, or incomplete records in the retrospective arm):

```
n_recruit = 30 / (1 − 0.20) = 30 / 0.80 = 37.5 ≈ 38 patients
```

Accordingly, the study aims to **recruit 38 patients** in order to retain a minimum of **30 evaluable patients** at final analysis. This target provides adequate statistical power while remaining consistent with the institutional revision TKR volume during the study period. All consecutive eligible patients will be enrolled (universal/consecutive sampling).

- **Retrospective arm:** Patients who underwent revision TKR from January 2020 to January 2025 with available records.
- **Prospective arm:** Patients undergoing revision TKR from February 2025 to December 2026.

Statistical advice has been sought from the Department of Preventive and Social Medicine (Biostatistics), Seth G.S. Medical College and KEM Hospital. The final sample size and power assumptions will be confirmed with the institutional biostatistician prior to IEC submission.

> **Note on power approximation.** The above uses the normal (Z) approximation. The exact paired *t*-test (based on the non-central *t*-distribution) typically requires one to two additional subjects for the same power; the quoted figure of ~80% should therefore be read as approximate, and the 38-patient recruitment target provides a comfortable margin.

**5.6 INCLUSION CRITERIA:**

1. All patients who underwent revision total knee replacement (single-stage or two-stage) at our institution during the study period.
2. Patients aged 18 years or above.
3. Minimum follow-up of 6 months from the date of revision surgery (or from Stage 2 reimplantation in two-stage cases).
4. Patients willing to provide written informed consent.

**5.7 EXCLUSION CRITERIA:**

1. Patients with incomplete medical records or unavailable radiographs (retrospective arm).
2. Patients lost to follow-up before completing 6 months.
3. Patients unwilling to participate or unable to provide consent.
4. Mega-prosthesis or tumour prosthesis reconstruction cases.
5. Isolated polyethylene liner exchange without revision of any metal component.
6. Arthrodesis conversion cases.

**5.8 OPERATIONAL DEFINITIONS:**

1. **Revision TKR:** Any surgical procedure involving exchange, removal, or addition of one or more components of a previously implanted total knee prosthesis.
2. **Aseptic Loosening:** Failure of fixation in the absence of infection, evidenced by progressive radiolucent lines (>2 mm), component migration (>2 mm), or alignment change (>3°), with negative infection workup.
3. **Periprosthetic Joint Infection (PJI):** Defined per the 2018 ICM/MSIS criteria. Major criteria: two positive periprosthetic cultures with identical organisms OR a sinus tract communicating with the joint. Minor criteria include elevated serum CRP/ESR, elevated synovial WBC count, elevated synovial PMN percentage, positive alpha-defensin, elevated synovial CRP, and positive histology.
4. **Radiological Loosening:** Progressive radiolucent lines >2 mm in all zones, component migration/subsidence >2 mm, alignment change >3° on serial radiographs, or cement mantle fracture.
5. **Instability:** Symptomatic AP translation >10 mm or ML laxity >10° causing functional impairment.
6. **Stiffness/Arthrofibrosis:** ROM <90° flexion or flexion contracture >10° limiting function, without mechanical or infective cause.
7. **Clinical Success:** KSS improvement of ≥10 points (MCID) from baseline, stable well-fixed implant, and no re-revision at latest follow-up.
8. **Clinical Failure:** Need for re-revision for any cause, OR radiological loosening, OR persistent pain with KSS improvement below MCID.
9. **Re-revision:** Any subsequent surgical procedure on the same knee involving component exchange, removal, or addition after the index revision.
10. **Patient Satisfaction:** Measured on a validated 5-point Likert scale; total score ≥15/25 = Satisfied, <15/25 = Not Satisfied.
11. **Minimum Follow-up:** Six months from revision surgery (or Stage 2 reimplantation in two-stage cases).

**5.11 DATA COLLECTION:**

**A. PREOPERATIVE PARAMETERS:** Demographics (age, sex, BMI, comorbidities [DM, HTN, IHD, RA, CKD, hypothyroidism], ASA grade, smoking/tobacco history); primary surgery details (date, side, indication, implant type and brand, fixation method, hospital, previous revisions, time interval to revision); indication for revision (aseptic loosening, PJI, instability, periprosthetic fracture, wear, malalignment, stiffness, extensor mechanism failure, unexplained pain); clinical assessment (ROM, stability [AP/ML], extensor mechanism, walking aid, KSS, WOMAC, VAS pain); radiological assessment (AP standing, lateral, skyline, full-length scanogram; mechanical axis; α/β/γ/δ angles; radiolucent lines [KS zones]; AORI bone defect classification; joint line position; Insall-Salvati ratio); laboratory investigations (CBC, ESR, CRP, serum albumin, HbA1c, urea/creatinine, coagulation profile; joint aspirate when PJI suspected).

**B. INTRAOPERATIVE PARAMETERS:** Anaesthesia type, tourniquet use/duration; surgical approach (medial parapatellar, subvastus, lateral, TTO, quadriceps snip, V-Y turndown) and reason for extensile exposure; intraoperative findings (soft tissue condition, collateral/PCL status, extensor mechanism, metallosis, synovitis, osteolytic cavities, membrane samples); bone defect (intraoperative AORI confirmation for femur and tibia, patellar bone stock); implant (brand/model, constraint level [PS/CCK/RHK], component sizes, polyethylene thickness/type, patella management); stems (femoral and tibial; cemented/press-fit; length; diameter); augments (distal femoral, posterior condylar, tibial; sizes); metaphyseal fixation (sleeves, tantalum cones); bone grafting; cement (plain vs antibiotic-loaded); for two-stage cases (Stage 1 findings, spacer type, organism, interim antibiotics, serial ESR/CRP, repeat aspirate, Stage 2 findings/cultures); operative time, estimated blood loss, intraoperative complications.

**C. POSTOPERATIVE PARAMETERS:** Immediate (0–6 weeks): hospital stay, ICU admission, mobilization day, weight-bearing status, drain output, transfusion, postoperative haemoglobin, DVT prophylaxis, wound status, rehabilitation milestones, discharge details. Follow-up schedule: 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, latest follow-up. Clinical assessment at each visit (ROM, KSS, WOMAC, VAS, stability, extensor mechanism, walking aid, stair climbing, ability to squat/sit cross-legged). Radiological assessment at each visit (AP/lateral radiographs; scanogram at 6 months and 1 year; component alignment; radiolucent lines [progressive vs non-progressive]; signs of loosening; joint line position; patellar tracking; heterotopic ossification [Brooker]). Complications: early and late. Re-revision: date, indication, interval, procedure. Mortality: cause and relation to surgery.

**Observer reliability:** All radiological measurements will be performed independently by two observers (the co-investigator and a senior orthopaedic surgeon). Inter-observer and intra-observer reliability will be assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen's Kappa to minimize measurement bias.

**5.13 STATISTICAL ANALYSIS:**

Data analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.).

- **Descriptive statistics:** Mean ± SD (continuous, normally distributed); median with IQR (non-normal); frequencies and percentages (categorical).
- **Normality testing:** Shapiro-Wilk test.
- **Paired t-test / Wilcoxon signed-rank test:** Pre- vs post-operative scores.
- **Independent t-test / Mann-Whitney U test:** Between two subgroups.
- **One-way ANOVA / Kruskal-Wallis test:** Between more than two groups, with post-hoc analysis.
- **Chi-square / Fisher's exact test:** Categorical associations.
- **Pearson's / Spearman's correlation:** Between continuous variables.
- **Multiple linear regression / multivariate logistic regression:** Independent predictors of outcomes and satisfaction (variables with p < 0.1 on univariate entered into model).
- **Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank test:** Implant survivorship with re-revision as endpoint.
- **Significance level:** p < 0.05; 95% confidence intervals reported.

## 10. LITERATURE REVIEW

### 10.1 Epidemiology of Revision TKR

Total knee replacement is among the most commonly performed orthopaedic procedures worldwide, with over 1.5 million procedures annually. As primary TKR is increasingly performed in younger, more active patients, and life expectancy rises, the burden of revision TKR continues to grow. Kurtz et al. (2007) projected a 601% increase in revision TKR procedures in the United States by 2030. The Australian Joint Registry (AOANJRR 2023) reports a cumulative revision rate of approximately 6.5% at 15 years following primary TKR, while the National Joint Registry of England and Wales (NJR 2023) recorded a continuing rise in revision procedures. In India, the absence of a national joint registry limits epidemiological data, although high-volume tertiary centres report a steadily rising revision burden.

### 10.2 Indications for Revision TKR

The indications for revision TKR have been documented across multiple registries and recent studies. An updated worldwide registry analysis (2024) identified the commonest causes of TKA revision as deep infection, aseptic loosening, instability, and pain, noting that septic complications are rising while aseptic loosening is declining. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis similarly reported periprosthetic joint infection (22.3%) and aseptic loosening (20.5%) as the leading etiologies.

A tertiary-centre study by Stroobant et al. (2025) found that while infection remained the leading cause of primary revisions, aseptic loosening was the predominant indication for re-revision, followed by PJI and instability. This temporal pattern—infection driving early failure and aseptic loosening driving late failure—is consistent across the literature.

### 10.3 Indications in Emerging Economies — Relevance to the Indian Context

A critical observation for the present study is the difference in revision indications between high-income countries and emerging economies. Whereas Western registries show infection and aseptic loosening contributing roughly equal proportions (each approximately 20–30%), data from emerging economies demonstrates that infection is a far more dominant cause of failure, reported in some series at over 70%, with infection being the most common reason for early revision. This marked difference underscores the importance of generating region-specific outcome data, which is a principal aim of this study.

### 10.4 Bone Defect Classification (AORI — Engh & Ammeen, 1999)

| Type | Femur | Tibia | Management |
|------|-------|-------|------------|
| Type 1 | Intact metaphysis, minor defects | Intact metaphysis, minor defects | Cement fill, small augments |
| Type 2A | One condyle damaged | One plateau damaged | Metal augments + stem |
| Type 2B | Both condyles damaged | Both plateaux damaged | Augments + stem + sleeve/cone |
| Type 3 | Deficient metaphysis | Deficient metaphysis | Sleeve/cone/allograft/hinge |

This classification guides intraoperative decision-making regarding implant constraint, augments, and reconstruction strategy.

### 10.5 Clinical and Functional Outcomes

Revision TKR reliably improves clinical and functional scores compared with the preoperative state, though outcomes generally remain inferior to primary TKR. A matched comparison (Salimy et al., 2023) demonstrated that patients undergoing revision TKR consistently report worse patient-reported outcome measures than primary TKR patients, with lower rates of meaningful improvement; Lim et al. (2025) further showed differences in the time taken to achieve the MCID between primary and revision TKA.

Long-term data is encouraging in selected cases. A study of first revision TKR at a mean 11-year follow-up reported functional and pain scores in the medium range with satisfaction in the upper third, and notably found no significant differences in outcome between one- and two-stage revisions or between septic and aseptic revisions.

### 10.6 Patient Satisfaction

Patient satisfaction following revision TKR is consistently lower than after primary TKR. Recent primary TKA series report satisfaction approaching 90%. In contrast, a dedicated satisfaction study of revision TKR (2023) found that approximately 85% of patients were satisfied and would undergo the procedure again, with a mean satisfaction score of 8.17 on a 10-point scale. A comparative study of 2,151 primary and 235 aseptic revision procedures (Winther et al., 2023) confirmed a significantly higher dissatisfaction rate following revision at one-year follow-up.

A cohort undergoing aseptic revision TKR (2021) identified aseptic loosening, instability, and implant failure as the commonest indications, providing useful PROM benchmarks for comparison. Established predictors of dissatisfaction in the literature include persistent pain, limited range of motion, and unmet preoperative expectations.

### 10.7 Indian Literature and Gap Analysis

Published Indian data on comprehensive outcomes following revision TKR remains extremely limited. Rajgopal et al. (2014) reported improvement in Knee Society Score from 38 to 74 at three years in a series of 45 revision TKRs. However, no published Indian study has simultaneously evaluated clinical, radiological, and patient satisfaction outcomes, and no outcome data exists from KEM Hospital, one of the highest-volume arthroplasty centres in Maharashtra.

**Rationale for the present study:**

1. Rising burden of revision TKR in India with no national registry data.
2. Distinct indication profile in emerging economies (infection-dominant) compared with Western data, necessitating region-specific evidence.
3. Absence of comprehensive Indian studies combining clinical, radiological, and satisfaction outcomes.
4. No published outcome data from a high-volume centre such as KEM Hospital.
5. Need to characterise Indian-specific factors—younger age at primary surgery, higher infection burden, delayed presentation, and differing patient expectations.
